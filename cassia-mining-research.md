# Cassia County, Idaho — Mining Research Report

*Compiled 2026-07-29 · companion to `cassia-mineral-monitor.html` (the interactive map)*

## What this is

Four independent government datasets — USGS MRDS mineral-site records, USGS USMIN mine features digitized off historical topo maps, BLM MLRS mining-claim records, and the Idaho Geological Survey's Mines and Prospects database — were pulled for Cassia County, cross-checked, cleaned, and fused into one interactive map, then grounded in the county's primary historical record: Anderson's 1931 Bulletin 14, the Idaho Mine Inspector's annual reports (1920–1955), and the USGS bulletins of the 1910s. The result is a picture of every place in the county where minerals were found, dug, claimed, or quarried, from the 1880 silver discoveries to Liberty Gold's current permit filings.

## The county in one paragraph

Cassia County was never a famous metal county, and knowing that is what makes it prospectable: the record is small enough to read completely. Silver–lead–zinc lodes were found around 1880 in two separated ranges — the Stokes district in the Albion Range and the Black Pine district in the Black Pine Mountains — and stayed small; Anderson (1931) summed up fifty years of work with "the production has been small." Snake River "flour gold" placers along the county's northern edge posted the largest county share on the upper Snake ($57,408 recorded, 1902–15) before Milner Dam drowned the bars in 1905. The county's real mining industry turned out to be rock, not metal: the Oakley stone (micaceous quartzite) quarries on Middle Mountain have shipped dimension stone continuously since 1948 and move roughly 50,000 tons a year today. And then the modern twist — Pegasus Gold's Black Pine open pit produced 435,000 oz of gold from 1991–1997, and Liberty Gold's Black Pine oxide-gold project now carries a 3.11 Moz probable reserve, entered federal EIS permitting with an April 2026 Notice of Intent, and targets first gold in 2028. That project is why the southeast corner of the claims map is a solid wall of teal.

## How the sources hook together

Each dataset answers a different question, and the map layers them so the answers corroborate. MRDS (247 records after excluding two known coordinate errors) says *what was reported found here* — commodities, development status, workings. IGS DD-1 (125 records) is the state's own file on the same ground, with PLSS locations, company names, and district tags; where an MRDS dot and an IGS dot coincide, that's two agencies agreeing a deposit exists. USMIN (279 points, 49 polygons) says *what was physically dug* — every adit, shaft, prospect pit, quarry, and open pit a USGS topographer drew on a quad sheet between 1958 and 2001; these are the features you can actually walk to (and should never enter). BLM MLRS says *who believes there's still something worth owning*: 1,497 active or freshly filed claims and 4,734 closed ones, the closed set being a heat map of every spot that excited somebody enough to stake it since the 1970s. The seven district markers carry the historical narrative that explains the clusters. Symbology follows the original spec: circles are existing or producing operations, dots are old mines and prospects, and marker color is the commodity class.

## The seven districts

**Black Pine (southeast corner, T15–16S R29–30E, near the Utah line).** The county's main event. Epithermal silver–zinc–mercury replacement bodies in Mississippian limestone were located around 1880 — Silver Hills shipped ore from 1894, average assays ran 42 oz/ton silver with zinc, lead, and antimony, and cinnabar was prospected at Juniper Hill. Noranda recognized disseminated Carlin-style gold in the 1980s; Pegasus mined the A, B, CD, E and Tolman pits 1991–97 (435 koz Au, 189 koz Ag) and went bankrupt in 1998. Liberty Gold consolidated the ground in 2016, drilled it into a 4.16 Moz indicated resource, and is now in FAST-41 EIS permitting — feasibility study due Q4 2026. Practical upshot: the district core is comprehensively claimed (the densest township, T15S R29E, holds 460 active claims), so treat it as look-don't-stake ground; the historic workings on its fringes are the interesting hiking.

**Stokes (Albion Range, Conner Creek ~7 mi south of Albion, plus Fairchild/Boulder creeks and Old Dominion).** Mesothermal silver-lead quartz veins clustered around a small granite stock in Precambrian quartzite, worked at 7,000–8,200 ft. The Melcher (Cumora) mine was the serious operation — 4,900 ft of tunnels, a tram, and a flotation mill by the 1910s, idle after 1920. The 1929 Inspector's report lists the whole district as "annual labor only," and it never revived. Old dumps at elevation, unclaimed except for scattered modern lode claims west of Albion.

**Dolomite (south of Oakley, against the Utah line).** The Idaho side of the silver belt whose star, the Vipont mine, sits two miles into Box Elder County, Utah (roughly a million ounces of silver in 1922 alone). Oakley-based companies — Idaho Silver Mining Co. among them — held claims straddling the line in the 1920s and drove a few hundred feet of workings on the Idaho side. No recorded Idaho production, which means the belt's Idaho extension was tested but never disproven; the 1921–29 Inspector reports are the only written record this district existed at all.

**Goose Creek (southwest plateau).** Lignite in Miocene–Pliocene lake beds: the Worthington mine ran one winter around 1907, hauling coal 25 miles by team to Oakley before everyone admitted the fuel was terrible (34% moisture, 5,810 Btu). The AEC came back in 1953–55 because the same carbonaceous beds carry uranium (USGS TEI-339/438); oil ventures drilled the basin 1919–29 and found only artesian water. A curiosity district — go for the geology, not riches.

**Oakley stone / Middle Mountain (8 mi south of Oakley) — ACTIVE.** About a dozen operators quarry foliated micaceous Elba Quartzite that splits into natural veneer and flagstone; Northern Stone Supply alone works ~1,300 acres and ships ~50,000 t/yr. This is private, producing ground — the circles on the map you can't prospect but can appreciate: it is the most commercially important mining ever done in the county.

**City of Rocks – Almo area.** Pegmatite mica, feldspar and beryl pits fringing the Oligocene Almo granite; 1950s uranium staking (the "Wicked Witch" prospect) and monazite black-sand placer evaluations along Edwards, Graham and Circle creeks in the Almo basin — the county's only tungsten showing (Black Jack Scheelite) is here too. The core has been closed to mineral entry since the National Reserve was established in 1988; NPS inventories seven abandoned-mineral-land features inside. The black-sand creeks *outside* the Reserve boundary are a legitimately under-examined corner — MRDS lists monazite as a primary commodity there.

**Snake River – Raft River placers (northern edge).** Flour gold so fine that 1,000–2,000 colors made a cent, worked on skim bars from the 1880s until Milner Dam (1905) and the Minidoka reservoir drowned the ground. The Sweetzer Farm placer near the Raft River mouth is a recorded producer. Panning the surviving reach is a pleasant way to confirm the gold is real and unrecoverable at scale.

## Reading the claims picture

Of 1,497 active-status claims, 899 are fully active and 598 are newly filed/pending — the filings wave tracks Liberty Gold's project maturing. Lode claims dominate (1,406 vs 82 placer and 9 mill sites). Two areas hold almost everything: T15S–16S R29–30E (Black Pine) and T15S–16S R22E on the west side of the Albion Range near the stone quarries. The 4,734 closed claims are the prospector's layer: each is ground someone once paid to stake, later dropped — usually open to relocation unless re-staked, withdrawn, or private. BLM's public GIS does not publish claimant names or location dates; to see who holds a claim, search its serial number (format `ID10xxxxxxx`) in the MLRS public reports at mlrs.blm.gov. Claim polygons are snapped to PLSS sections and aliquot parts — legal boundaries exist only as monuments on the ground.

Caveats that matter in the field: MRDS coordinates can be off by hundreds of meters, and its "Producer" flags reflect the last update, not today; two MRDS records with demonstrably wrong coordinates (Butte County sites plotted inside Cassia) were excluded. USMIN features may be collapsed, reclaimed, or fenced since the map date shown on each record. MRDS's biggest single commodity classes here are construction sand & gravel (89 sites) and geothermal wells/springs (41) — filter those off in the map when metal hunting. Geothermal, incidentally, is the county's quiet mineral-economy sleeper (the Raft River KGRA sits just east), but it's outside prospecting scope.

## The paper trail

The closest thing to "journal entries of the mines" is the Idaho Mine Inspector's *Annual Report of the Mining Industry of Idaho*, scanned in full at idahogeology.org (`/Uploads/Data/ISMIR/<year>_ISMIR.pdf`) — the 1920, 1921, 1922, 1929 and 1955 volumes carry the richest Cassia sections, down to how many men each company employed. The foundational document is Anderson, *Geology and Mineral Resources of Eastern Cassia County* (Idaho Bureau of Mines and Geology Bulletin 14, 1931), a property-by-property census of every working then known. For specific threads: Bowen, USGS Bulletin 531-H (1913) on the Goose Creek lignite; Hill, USGS Bulletin 620-L (1916) on the Snake River flour gold; Mapel & Hail, TEI-339 (1953) on uranium in the lignite; Miller et al., USGS OFR 2008-1103 for modern Almo/City of Rocks geology; the NPS Geologic Resources Inventory (2010) for Reserve mine features; and Liberty Gold's project page and April 2026 NOI release for the current state of Black Pine. Every one of these is linked, with working URLs, in the map's Reading Room panel and in `data/history_notes.md`.

## Safety and legality

Never enter adits, shafts, or open workings — bad air, unstable ground, and vertical drops are how prospectors die, and USMIN features are 25–70 years stale. An active claim is a property right: sampling on one without the claimant's permission is claim jumping. Verify surface ownership (BLM vs. state vs. private — much of the county's low ground is private ranch land) before digging; City of Rocks National Reserve is closed to collecting; the Oakley quarries are private businesses. This compilation is a research aid, not a legal land-status document.
